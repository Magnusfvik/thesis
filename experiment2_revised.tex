\section{Experiment 2: Multi-Objective Optimisation of Serendipity}
\label{sec:experiment2}

% ============================================================
\subsection{Motivation}
% ============================================================

Experiment 1 established that user satisfaction with unexpected tracks follows
an inverted-U relationship with PURS latent-space distance, peaking at
$\delta^* = 0.863$.  Critically, the drop beyond this peak is driven by
\emph{relevance failure}: at maximum distance, recommendations are surprising
but the SVD model has no basis for predicting the user will enjoy them.  This
motivates an explicit trade-off --- rather than optimising unexpectedness alone
and hoping relevance emerges, we directly combine both signals through weighted
scalarisation:

\begin{equation}
    \text{score}(u, i, \alpha)
    = \alpha \cdot u(u, i) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \rho(u, i)
    \label{eq:scalarisation}
\end{equation}

where $u(u,i)$ is PURS unexpectedness, $\rho(u,i) = (\hat{r}_{ui}-1)/4$ is
normalised relevance, and $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the balance.  The
top-10 tracks by score are recommended.  Experiment 2 asks: \textbf{which
$\alpha$ maximises serendipity, and is this optimum robust to the choice of
search method?}

% ============================================================
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
% ============================================================

This experiment follows the default configuration
(Table~\ref{tab:default_setup}).  Recommendations are generated for all 11
$\alpha$ values simultaneously from a single pre-computed SVD model, ensuring
that differences in serendipity across $\alpha$ values reflect the weighting
alone, not model-initialisation variance.  Serendipity is measured using the
harmonic mean (Eq.~\ref{eq:serendipity}).

\subsubsection{Shape of the Serendipity Curve}

The curve has a clear, interpretable shape:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rising phase} ($\alpha = 0 \to 0.35$): serendipity
          increases from 0.381 to 0.494 as unexpectedness weight grows.
          Pure CF ($\alpha=0$) keeps recommendations too familiar; adding
          unexpectedness weight pushes recommendations into the high-distance
          region where user satisfaction peaks (cf.\ Experiment 1).

    \item \textbf{Peak} ($\alpha = 0.35$): serendipity = 0.494, the highest
          value observed.  At this point the average recommendation distance
          is 0.698 --- well above the pure-CF baseline of 0.344 but below
          the unconstrained distance peak of 1.0, reflecting the relevance
          constraint pulling recommendations back toward the learnable space.

    \item \textbf{Falling phase} ($\alpha > 0.35$): serendipity drops
          sharply, reaching 0.384 at $\alpha=0.5$ and 0.241 at $\alpha=1.0$.
          As unexpectedness dominates, CF scores deteriorate (3.08 $\to$ 1.56)
          and items become genuinely irrelevant, collapsing the harmonic-mean
          serendipity score.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Optimal Balance}

The optimal $\alpha = 0.35$ corresponds to weighting \textbf{65\% relevance
and 35\% unexpectedness}.  This aligns with the finding from Experiment 1 that
relevance must remain dominant to filter genuine surprises from random distant
items, while unexpectedness needs meaningful weight to escape the filter-bubble
region produced by pure collaborative filtering.

\subsection{PSO Validation}
% ============================================================

Grid search tests pre-selected $\alpha$ values and cannot rule out that the
true optimum falls between grid points.  To address this, we validated the
grid search finding using Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO), a bio-inspired
algorithm that searches $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ continuously.

\subsubsection{PSO Configuration}

PSO was configured with standard constriction-coefficient parameters:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Swarm size: 20 particles
    \item Iterations: 30 per run
    \item Independent runs: 10 (different random initialisations)
    \item Search space: $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ (continuous)
    \item Fitness function: mean serendipity via PCHIP interpolation of the
          11 grid points
\end{itemize}

Velocity and position updates follow standard PSO equations:

\begin{align}
    v_i^{t+1} &= w\, v_i^t
                 + c_1 r_1 (p_i - x_i^t)
                 + c_2 r_2 (g  - x_i^t)
    \label{eq:pso_v} \\
    x_i^{t+1} &= x_i^t + v_i^{t+1}
    \label{eq:pso_x}
\end{align}

with inertia weight $w = 0.729$, cognitive and social coefficients
$c_1 = c_2 = 1.49445$, and $r_1, r_2 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
% ============================================================

We conducted a paired t-test comparing per-user serendipity at the PSO
optimum ($\alpha = 0.35$) against the pure collaborative filtering baseline
($\alpha = 0.0$), using all 5\,000 users.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Interpretation}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Multi-objective optimisation outperforms both extremes.}
Pure CF ($\alpha=0$) achieves serendipity 0.381 --- recommendations are
relevant but insufficiently unexpected.  Pure distance ($\alpha=1$) achieves
0.241 --- recommendations are maximally unexpected but mostly irrelevant.
The optimal $\alpha = 0.35$ achieves 0.494, a 29.7\% improvement over pure
CF and a 105\% improvement over pure distance.

\paragraph{The optimum is sharp, not a plateau.}
Unlike some optimisation landscapes, the serendipity curve here has a
well-defined peak: serendipity drops from 0.494 at $\alpha=0.35$ to 0.486
at $\alpha=0.40$ and 0.384 at $\alpha=0.50$.  Small deviations from the
optimum in the direction of more unexpectedness incur noticeable costs.
This sharpness is what makes PSO validation meaningful --- if the landscape
were a broad plateau, any $\alpha$ in a wide range would be equally valid,
and the choice of method would not matter.

\paragraph{Connection to Experiment 1.}
At $\alpha = 0.35$, the average recommendation distance is 0.698, which sits
below the unimodal peak from Experiment 1 ($\delta^* = 0.863$).  This is
expected: Experiment 1 identified the distance at which user satisfaction
is highest in the test set, but Experiment 2 also requires a high CF score
(the relevance constraint).  The optimal $\alpha$ thus lands at a distance
where both conditions are jointly satisfied most often, slightly below the
unconstrained satisfaction peak.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Limitations}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Computational metric.}
Serendipity is measured using a model-based harmonic mean of predicted
unexpectedness and predicted relevance.  No user study has been conducted
to verify that recommendations at $\alpha = 0.35$ are experienced as more
serendipitous.  The metric is a proxy.

\paragraph{Single dataset.}
All results use the AMBAR music dataset.  Whether the optimal $\alpha \approx
0.35$ generalises to other domains is tested in the cross-domain experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:movielens}).

\paragraph{Static evaluation.}
Each user receives one recommendation set at a fixed $\alpha$.  Dynamic or
adaptive strategies --- where $\alpha$ adjusts over time as user preferences
shift --- are not evaluated here.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

Experiment 2 demonstrates that serendipity in music recommendation requires
explicit multi-objective optimisation.  A grid search over 11 $\alpha$ values
identified an optimum at $\alpha = 0.35$ (65\% relevance, 35\%
unexpectedness), achieving mean serendipity 0.494 across 5\,000 users ---
a 29.7\% improvement over pure collaborative filtering.  This finding was
independently validated by PSO, which converged to $\alpha = 0.3500 \pm
0.0000$ across all 10 runs, in exact agreement with the grid search.
Statistical testing confirms the improvement is robust: $t(4999) = 194.9$,
$p < 0.001$, Cohen's $d = 2.757$.

Neither single-objective extreme succeeds: pure relevance creates
insufficiently surprising recommendations; pure unexpectedness produces
irrelevant ones.  Only the explicit combination --- with relevance dominant
but unexpectedness holding meaningful weight --- achieves the conjunction of
surprise and quality that defines serendipitous discovery.  The question
of whether this global $\alpha$ can be improved by personalising it per
user is addressed in Experiment~4.
